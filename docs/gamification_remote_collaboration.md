# Gamifying Knowledge Sharing and Team Collaboration in Remote Work

## 1. Recommended Topic

**Gamifying Knowledge Sharing and Team Collaboration in Remote Work**

This topic focuses on communication barriers, limited knowledge sharing, and reduced team connectedness among remote employees.

It is suitable for a gamification project because the target behaviours—communication, knowledge exchange, and collaboration—can be demonstrated and measured through an interactive classroom game.

## 2. Business Problem

Remote employees often have fewer opportunities for informal conversations and spontaneous knowledge exchange. Important information may remain with individual employees rather than being shared across the team. This can weaken collaboration and employees' sense of connection.

### Research supporting the problem

A 2026 study, *Beyond Where We Work: Daily Informal Communication, Knowledge Sharing, and Commitment in Hybrid Teams*, investigated 219 hybrid employees across 1,655 daily observations. It reported that remote-work days were associated with less informal communication and knowledge sharing, with related implications for team commitment.

- Study: https://doi.org/10.3390/admsci16020063

### Business challenge

Imagine a company whose employees work remotely. They face three challenges:

1. **Communication barriers:** Employees rely heavily on scheduled meetings and written messages.
2. **Poor knowledge sharing:** Useful information remains distributed among individual employees.
3. **Reduced team engagement:** Employees may feel less connected to colleagues and less involved in team activities.

### Project objective

> How can gamification encourage knowledge sharing, improve communication, and strengthen collaboration among remote employees?

## 3. Game Concept: Remote Mission – Save the Project

### Overview

Participants become employees of a fictional company working remotely. Their team must solve a business problem, but each employee possesses different information. No individual can solve the challenge alone.

- **Participants:** 8–16 classmates
- **Teams:** 2–4 teams of four players
- **Duration:** Approximately 12 minutes
- **Materials:** Printed role cards, mission cards, timer, and scoreboard

### Step 1: Assign roles

Each participant receives a role and private information.

| Player | Role | Information |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Sales Manager | Customer requirements |
| 2 | Financial Manager | Budget limitations |
| 3 | IT Manager | Technical possibilities |
| 4 | Project Manager | Project deadlines and resources |

Players cannot show their cards to one another. They must communicate the information verbally or through the permitted communication channel.

### Step 2: Introduce the business challenge

The company must organize an online event for an important client and select an appropriate platform.

The requirements are:

- At least 100 participants.
- The event must take place on Friday.
- The budget must not exceed €300.
- Setup must be completed before Friday.

### Step 3: Distribute information

**Player 1 – Sales Manager**

- The client requires at least 100 participants.
- The event must take place on Friday.

**Player 2 – Financial Manager**

The available budget is €300.

| Platform | Price |
|---|---:|
| Platform A | €250 |
| Platform B | €100 |
| Platform C | €350 |

**Player 3 – IT Manager**

| Platform | Capacity | Setup time |
|---|---:|---|
| Platform A | 120 participants | 2 days |
| Platform B | 80 participants | 1 day |
| Platform C | 150 participants | 1 day |

**Player 4 – Project Manager**

- The technical team has Wednesday and Thursday available for preparation.
- Setup must be completed before Friday.

### Step 4: Play the mission

Teams have three minutes to exchange information and agree on a solution. Each team submits:

1. Its selected platform.
2. The reasons for its selection.
3. An explanation of how the choice meets the business requirements.

**Correct answer: Platform A.** It meets the capacity and budget requirements, and its two-day setup can be completed on Wednesday and Thursday.

## 4. Gamification Mechanics

| Game mechanic | Implementation | Intended business objective |
|---|---|---|
| Quests | Teams complete business challenges | Encourage collaboration |
| Points | Award points for successful missions and useful contributions | Reinforce participation |
| Badges | Recognize cooperation and knowledge sharing | Encourage helpful behaviour |
| Progress bar | Display completed missions | Make progress visible |
| Team competition | Teams compare results | Add a shared challenge |
| Levels | Missions become progressively harder | Maintain an appropriate challenge |
| Feedback | Teams receive immediate results | Support learning |

### Example scoring system

- Correct solution: **+100 points**
- Every team member contributes relevant information: **+40 points**
- Correct explanation: **+30 points**
- Completion within the time limit: **+20 points**

The scoring system should prioritize cooperation and accuracy rather than speed alone. The goal is to encourage communication and knowledge sharing, not simply faster task completion.

## 5. Two-Round Classroom Playtest

A two-round playtest allows participants to experience a non-gamified and a gamified collaboration task.

### Round 1: Without gamification

**Duration: 3 minutes**

Teams solve a business challenge without game mechanics:

- No points or achievements.
- No progress tracking.
- No narrative or mission structure.

### Round 2: With gamification

**Duration: 3 minutes**

Teams solve a different challenge of comparable difficulty, with:

- A mission narrative.
- Team points.
- Achievements.
- Progress tracking.
- Immediate feedback.
- Cooperative objectives.

### Evaluation

After both rounds, ask participants to rate the experience and record task outcomes.

| Measurement | Round 1 | Round 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Engagement (1–5) | To be measured | To be measured |
| Enjoyment (1–5) | To be measured | To be measured |
| Perceived collaboration (1–5) | To be measured | To be measured |
| Task completion time | To be measured | To be measured |
| Successful task completion | Yes/No | Yes/No |

This provides preliminary classroom feedback on whether the gamified version encouraged participation, perceived collaboration, and enjoyment.

**Methodological limitation:** A short classroom exercise cannot establish that gamification reduces long-term social isolation or improves workplace performance. Differences may also result from task difficulty, group composition, or participants becoming familiar with the activity.

For a fairer comparison, have half the groups complete the gamified version first and the other half complete the non-gamified version first. Use tasks of comparable difficulty.

## 6. Theoretical Frameworks

### A. Self-Determination Theory (SDT)

Self-Determination Theory identifies three psychological needs relevant to motivation:

| Psychological need | Application in the game |
|---|---|
| Autonomy | Participants decide how to solve the challenge |
| Competence | Participants overcome challenges and receive feedback |
| Relatedness | Participants cooperate toward a shared objective |

Sailer et al. (2017) experimentally investigated how gamification elements influence psychological needs. Their findings indicate that elements such as teammates and meaningful narratives can support feelings of social relatedness.

- Sailer et al. (2017), *How Gamification Motivates*: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2016.12.033

### B. MDA Framework

MDA stands for Mechanics, Dynamics, and Aesthetics.

| Component | Application in the game |
|---|---|
| **Mechanics** | Points, badges, missions, and levels |
| **Dynamics** | Cooperation, communication, and decision-making |
| **Aesthetics** | Challenge, achievement, and social connection |

Example:

1. **Mechanic:** Participants must complete a mission using information distributed across four players.
2. **Dynamic:** Participants communicate and exchange knowledge.
3. **Aesthetic:** Participants experience teamwork, challenge, and accomplishment.

This framework explains how the designed mechanics are intended to encourage particular behaviours and experiences.

### C. Marczewski Player Types

Marczewski's player types could also be used to consider different participant motivations. The design should not assume that everyone is motivated by competition. Cooperative objectives, meaningful contributions, and opportunities to help others can accommodate a wider range of preferences.

## 7. Workplace Implementation

The classroom game is a prototype, not the complete workplace solution.

A real implementation could be a digital platform integrated into Microsoft Teams or Slack. Employees could participate in recurring cooperative challenges.

### Possible features

**Weekly Team Quests**

Employees complete collaborative missions together.

**Knowledge-Sharing Challenges**

Employees share useful insights, tips, or solutions with colleagues.

**Team Progress**

Departments work toward shared goals.

**Recognition**

Employees receive badges for helping colleagues and sharing knowledge.

### Intended impact model

Gamification mechanics  
↓  
Potentially increased motivation to participate  
↓  
More knowledge sharing and collaboration  
↓  
Stronger team connectedness  
↓  
Potential improvements in employee engagement and team effectiveness

These are intended outcomes and should be evaluated rather than assumed.

### Ethical considerations

Consider whether competition increases pressure or disadvantages quieter employees. The design should reward meaningful contributions rather than simply counting messages. Participation should be inclusive, transparent, and voluntary where possible. Employee monitoring should be proportionate and should not turn gamification into a tool for surveillance or coercion.

## 9. Final Project Recommendation

**Topic:** Gamifying Knowledge Sharing and Team Collaboration in Remote Work

- **Business problem:** Remote work can reduce informal communication and knowledge sharing, potentially weakening team connectedness.
- **Game:** Remote Mission – Save the Project.
- **Target players:** Employees working in distributed teams.
- **Main mechanics:** Cooperative quests, achievements, feedback, and shared progress.
- **Frameworks:** Self-Determination Theory, MDA, and Marczewski player types.
- **Prototype:** A cooperative business simulation played in class.
- **Playtest:** Compare a gamified and non-gamified collaborative task.

The main strength of this topic is that the presentation can demonstrate the problem and the proposed solution through an activity classmates actually experience. The playtest also provides feedback that can be discussed in the report.

### Suggested improvement

Simulate remote communication during the classroom game. For example, separate participants and require them to exchange information through written messages or a restricted communication channel. This more closely resembles remote work than ordinary face-to-face teamwork.
